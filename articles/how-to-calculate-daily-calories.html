How to Calculate Your Daily Calorie Needs

Health & Fitness · 6 min read

Every person burns energy differently based on height, weight, age, biological sex, and daily movement. Estimating your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) follows a transparent two-step process: first estimate how many calories your body needs at complete rest (Basal Metabolic Rate, or BMR), and then scale that resting figure by an activity multiplier.

Step 1: Estimate Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR)

Most modern dietetic tools rely on the Mifflin-St Jeor equation because it provides reliable baseline estimates across typical adult body compositions:

Mifflin-St Jeor Equation: Men: BMR = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) + 5
Women: BMR = (10 × kg) + (6.25 × cm) − (5 × age) − 161

Example (30-year-old woman, 65 kg, 165 cm):

BMR = (10 × 65) + (6.25 × 165) − (5 × 30) − 161 = 650 + 1,031.25 − 150 − 161 = 1,370 kcal/day.

Step 2: Multiply by Your Activity Level (TDEE)

Once you have your resting BMR, multiply it by the factor that best reflects your weekly routine—including non-exercise walking and desk work, not just gym workouts:

Activity Level Multiplier Typical Weekly Routine
Sedentary1.20Desk job, minimal deliberate exercise
Lightly Active1.375Light exercise or sports 1–3 days/week
Moderately Active1.55Moderate training 3–5 days/week
Very Active1.725Hard exercise 6–7 days/week or active job
Extra Active1.90Intense daily training or physical trade

Continuing the Example (Moderately Active):

TDEE = 1,370 × 1.55 ≈ 2,124 calories/day to maintain current body weight.

Step 3: Adjusting Calories Safely for Goals

  • Weight maintenance: Eat roughly near your estimated TDEE while monitoring average weight trends over 2–3 weeks.
  • Gradual fat loss: Moderate deficits of 300–500 calories below TDEE generally avoid unnecessary fatigue and muscle loss.
  • Lean muscle gain: Modest surpluses of 200–300 calories above TDEE pair well with progressive resistance training and adequate protein.
Medical & Nutrition Disclaimer: BMR and TDEE equations provide statistical estimates, not exact laboratory calorimetry. Individual metabolism varies with genetics, thyroid function, medications, and lean muscle mass. Consult a physician or registered dietitian before making major dietary changes or setting aggressive calorie deficits.
