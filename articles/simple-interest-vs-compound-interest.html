Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

Financial · 6 min read

Whether you are comparing savings accounts, certificates of deposit (GICs or term deposits), auto financing, or credit card balances, knowing whether interest is simple or compounded determines how fast money grows—or how expensive a debt becomes over time. Over short windows the gap looks modest, but across 10 to 30 years compounding creates a dramatic divergence.

1. What Is Simple Interest?

Simple interest is calculated strictly on the original principal amount (P). Past interest never earns additional interest, so growth follows a straight, predictable line year after year. Common uses include short-term promissory notes, treasury bills, and many simple-interest auto loans.

Simple Interest Formula: I = P × r × t   |   Total A = P × (1 + r × t)

Example ($10,000 at 6% Simple Interest for 5 Years):

Annual interest is constant at $10,000 × 0.06 = $600 per year. After 5 years, total interest equals $3,000 and the ending balance is $13,000.00.

2. What Is Compound Interest?

Compound interest adds each period's earned interest back into the balance, so future periods earn interest on both your original deposit and all previously accumulated interest. High-yield savings accounts, retirement portfolios, and credit cards rely on compounding.

Compound Interest Formula: A = P × (1 + r ÷ n)^(n × t)

Here n represents compounding periods per year (such as 12 for monthly or 365 for daily compounding).

3. Side-by-Side Comparison ($10,000 at 6% Annual Rate)

The table below compares $10,000 growing at 6% simple interest versus 6% compounded monthly (n = 12) with no extra deposits:

Time Horizon Simple Interest Balance Compound (Monthly) Extra Gain
1 Year $10,600.00 $10,616.78 +$16.78
5 Years $13,000.00 $13,488.50 +$488.50
10 Years $16,000.00 $18,193.97 +$2,193.97
20 Years $22,000.00 $33,102.04 +$11,102.04

4. Why APR and APY Differ

Banks frequently advertise both an interest rate (nominal APR) and an effective annual rate (APY or EAR). Because monthly compounding earns interest on interest during the year, a 6.00% nominal rate compounded monthly equals (1 + 0.06 / 12)^12 − 1 = 6.17% APY. When saving or investing, higher compounding frequency works in your favor; when carrying revolving debt, frequent compounding increases total borrowing cost.

Financial Disclaimer: Interest projections are mathematical illustrations for educational planning and exclude taxes, account fees, and variable rate changes. This guide does not constitute personalized financial or investment advice.

Conclusion

Simple interest favors borrowers on fixed short-term notes because accrued interest never snowballs, while compound interest rewards savers and long-term investors who give their money time to grow.
