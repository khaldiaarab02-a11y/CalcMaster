How to Calculate Profit Margin for Your Business

Business & Financial · 5 min read

Strong sales volume alone does not guarantee a healthy business. If product costs, shipping, payment processing fees, or discounts eat away at each order, revenue can rise while bank balances shrink. Measuring profit margin reveals how many cents of every revenue dollar remain as profit—and distinguishing margin from markup prevents costly retail pricing mistakes.

1. Gross Profit Margin Formula

Gross profit margin measures profitability after subtracting the direct cost of goods sold (COGS) or direct service delivery costs from revenue (selling price):

Gross Profit Margin: Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost   |   Margin (%) = (Gross Profit ÷ Revenue) × 100

Example (Retail Product):

A store buys a backpack wholesale for $36.00 and sells it for $60.00. Gross profit is $24.00, so gross profit margin equals ($24 ÷ $60) × 100 = 40.0%.

2. Profit Margin vs. Markup (Why Mixing Them Up Hurts Profits)

Markup divides profit by cost, whereas profit margin divides profit by selling price. Using the exact same $36 cost and $60 price:

  • Profit Margin: $24 ÷ $60 = 40.0%
  • Cost Markup: $24 ÷ $36 = 66.7%

If a shop owner wants a 40% margin on a $36 item and mistakenly marks up cost by 40% ($36 × 1.40 = $50.40), their actual profit margin turns out to be only ($14.40 ÷ $50.40) = 28.6%. To price directly for a target margin, use:

Target Margin Pricing Formula: Selling Price = Cost ÷ (1 − Target Margin Decimal)

3. Gross Margin vs. Net Profit Margin

While gross margin tracks direct unit economics, net profit margin reflects overall business profitability after deducting operating expenses—such as rent, payroll, software subscriptions, marketing spend, interest, and taxes. Pairing gross margin with your Break-Even Calculator shows exactly how many monthly sales are required to cover fixed overhead.

Business & Financial Note: Formulas here illustrate standard unit pricing and gross margin math. Always consult a qualified accountant or tax advisor for formal financial statements and tax reporting.

Conclusion

Keeping clear tabs on both profit margin and markup protects your cash flow as discounts, supplier rates, and ad costs fluctuate.
